# pm.perform — Performance management (projecten, track project, level 3, FADED examples, pushback on every lesson)
Index title: en 'Performance management' / nl 'Sturen op prestaties'. Blurb: en 'Indicators that steer, targets people believe, the performance conversation, gaming, and a review rhythm that lasts.' / nl 'Indicatoren die sturen, doelen waar mensen in geloven, het prestatiegesprek, gaming en een overlegritme dat blijft.'
Examples from a grid operator and a parts manufacturer. Not Lean, Kata or A3.
1. `pm.perform.kpis` — choosing a few indicators that steer; leading vs lagging; a written definition, one owner; pairing indicators.
2. `pm.perform.tree` — from strategy to a team’s numbers: KPI (driver) trees, checking a branch with data, cascading without copying.
3. `pm.perform.targets` — targets people believe: specific and hard but accepted (Locke and Latham), based on the team’s best weeks, ranges and triggers.
4. `pm.perform.dialogue` — the performance conversation: facts, cause, commitment; separating a one-off from a pattern.
5. `pm.perform.gaming` — Goodhart’s law, perverse incentives, warning signs, fixing the system rather than blaming people.
6. `pm.perform.rhythm` — a review cadence that survives the consultant leaving: cascade, frequency per number, fixed agenda, handover.

Why this split: the six existing lessons were all entry or working-knowledge material, so they fill levels 1–2 and the new lessons carry levels 3–4, except `smart` and `board`, which give the targets and rhythm topics a true entry level. KPIs and the tree start the indicators topic; definition work and strategy maps are what a performance consultant does next. Targets (Locke & Latham) sit at level 2 after SMART, with hoshin kanri and Deming’s challenge above. Dialogue and gaming start the behaviour topic, with Campbell (the work itself gets damaged) and coaching the leader above. Rhythm (review cascade and handover) sits at level 2 between the floor board and the monthly review; XmR charts close the module and answer the question the targets and monthly lessons leave open: when is red a real change?

Facts and sources (checked 2026-11-08):
- Operational definition: W. E. Deming, Out of the Crisis (MIT CAES, 1986), chapter on operational definitions. Taught as Deming’s idea, not quoted.
- Strategy maps: R. S. Kaplan & D. P. Norton, “Having trouble with your strategy? Then map it”, Harvard Business Review 78(5), 2000, 167–176; Strategy Maps (HBS Press, 2004). “Some twenty to twenty-five measures for a business unit’s scorecard” refers to Kaplan & Norton’s The Balanced Scorecard (1996), hedged as “mention”.
- H. Nørreklit, “The balance on the balanced scorecard – a critical analysis of some of its assumptions”, Management Accounting Research 11(1), 2000, 65–88: cause-and-effect links are logical or assumed rather than empirical, and the time dimension is missing. Summaries: https://maaw.info/ArticleSummaries/ArtSumKaplanNorton2000Map.htm, https://rd.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-030-38640-5_2.
- SMART: G. T. Doran, “There’s a S.M.A.R.T. way to write management’s goals and objectives”, Management Review 70(11), November 1981, 35–36: Specific, Measurable, Assignable, Realistic, Time-related; his remark that not every objective must be quantified at every level of management (quoted at https://rapidbi.com/?p=7850; history at https://www.projectsmart.co.uk/brief-history-of-smart-goals.php).
- Hoshin kanri: Y. Akao (ed.), Hoshin Kanri: Policy Deployment for Successful TQM (Productivity Press, 1991, English edition). Origin in 1960s Japanese TQM is the usual account; “three to five breakthrough objectives”, “weeks in the autumn” and the X-matrix are taught as practitioner practice, not as Akao’s rules; the X-matrix debate is stated as a debate.
- Deming’s point 11 (11a eliminate work standards/quotas; 11b eliminate management by objective, by numbers, numerical goals; substitute leadership): Out of the Crisis (1986); https://www.qualitydigest.com/inside/quality-insider-column/quotality.html, https://deming.org/?p=5280. “By what method?” taught as his question, paraphrased, not quoted.
- Joiner’s three ways (improve the system, distort the system, distort the data): B. L. Joiner, Fourth Generation Management (McGraw-Hill, 1994), also used by D. J. Wheeler.
- L. D. Ordóñez, M. E. Schweitzer, A. D. Galinsky & M. H. Bazerman, “Goals gone wild: the systematic side effects of overprescribing goal setting”, Academy of Management Perspectives 23(1), 2009, 6–16; Locke & Latham’s reply in the same volume (2009); rejoinder “On good scholarship, goal setting, and scholars gone wild”, AMP 23, 82–87 (https://repository.upenn.edu/mgmt_papers/325, https://hbswk.hbs.edu/item/on-good-scholarship-goal-setting-and-scholars-gone-wild).
- S. B. Sitkin, K. E. See, C. C. Miller, M. W. Lawless & A. M. Carton, “The paradox of stretch goals: organizations in pursuit of the seemingly impossible”, Academy of Management Review 36(3), 2011.
- Campbell’s law: D. T. Campbell, “Assessing the impact of planned social change”, Evaluation and Program Planning 2(1), 1979 (paper circulated from 1976); paraphrased, not quoted (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Campbell%27s_law).
- OSHA memo: R. Fairfax, “Employer safety incentive and disincentive policies and practices”, 12 March 2012 (https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/standardinterpretations/2012-03-12-0); now marked as an archive document, so the lesson only says OSHA “warned” in 2012.
- R. D. Austin, Measuring and Managing Performance in Organizations (Dorset House, 1996): informational vs motivational measurement; dysfunction when measures are incomplete.
- A. C. Edmondson, “Psychological safety and learning behavior in work teams”, Administrative Science Quarterly 44(2), 1999, 350–383; the earlier hospital study (Journal of Applied Behavioral Science, 1996): better nursing teams reported more medication errors, explained by willingness to report (https://explore.psychsafety.com/n/edmondson-1996/). The four zones (comfort, apathy, anxiety, learning) from her later work (The Fearless Organization, 2018).
- D. Mann, Creating a Lean Culture (Productivity Press, 3rd ed. 2015): four elements (leader standard work, visual controls, daily accountability, discipline) and hour-by-hour production tracking charts (https://www.leanblog.org/2014/12/podcast-212-david-mann-creating-a-lean-culture-3rd-ed). “Filled in by hand” taught as what many practitioners prefer.
- D. J. Wheeler, Understanding Variation (SPC Press, 1993; 2nd ed. 2000): process behaviour charts, XmR, natural process limits = mean ± 2.66 × average moving range (2.66 = 3 / d2 with d2 = 1.128 for n = 2; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shewhart_individuals_control_chart). “Eight in a row on one side” taught as a common rule; some sources use 7 or 9.
- Bowling chart, year-to-date, management by exception, countermeasure sheets, SQDC: common lean and hoshin practitioner terms, no single source; taught as practice.
