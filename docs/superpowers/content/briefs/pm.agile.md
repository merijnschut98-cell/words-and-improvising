# pm.agile — Agile and Scrum (projecten, track agile, level 2)

First module of the new Projecten track `agile`, "Agile and IT projects" / "Agile en IT-projecten" (plan: docs/superpowers/plans/2026-11-07-v13-listen-ict-outside-layout.md, section 3). Module `level: 2`, so for an unstarted pm learner `recommendedModule` still suggests pm.intake / pm.plan (level 1) first; Today picks for existing state are unchanged.

Format: topic module, 4 topics × 4 levels (Basis, Werkkennis, Gevorderd, Expert), as eng.machining. Worked examples at levels 1–2, faded at 3–4. ## Lean links (explicit where real, honest where different)

- Real: PDCA ≈ iteration; gemba ≈ showing working software; partially done work = inventory; over-processing = unused features; pull and WIP limits = Toyota kanban and buffers; Little’s law is the same law in factories; retrospective ≈ kaizen; Definition of Done ≈ quality gate; blocked card ≈ andon; refinement ≈ work preparation; SLE/age ≈ control-chart thinking; buffers/heijunka ≈ utilisation below 100%.
- Different: software makes something new each time, so less standard work; in product development variation is partly the point; a Toyota kanban signals a standard part, a Kanban card describes unique work; Lean “cycle time” (time per unit at a station) is not Kanban “cycle time” (start to finish of one item); variation in knowledge work is much larger than on a line.

## Facts and sources (verified 2026-11-07)

- Agile Manifesto: four values, closing line “while there is value in the items on the right, we value the items on the left more”; twelve principles (quoted verbatim for principles 1–3, 6–12). https://agilemanifesto.org/ and https://agilemanifesto.org/principles.html. Seventeen authors, Snowbird (Utah), February 2001 (agilemanifesto.org/history.html; KSL “The day Agile came to town”, https://www.ksl.com/article/50873737/). Some sources give 14 signatories at the meeting; the manifesto lists 17 authors, which is what the lessons say.
- Scrum Guide, November 2020 (https://scrumguides.org/scrum-guide.html): Scrum Team “typically 10 or fewer people”; accountabilities Product Owner, Scrum Master, Developers; “The Product Owner is one person, not a committee”; sprints “fixed length events of one month or less”; timeboxes for a one-month sprint: Sprint Planning max 8 h, Daily Scrum 15 min, Sprint Review max 4 h, Sprint Retrospective max 3 h, “for shorter Sprints, the event is usually shorter”; Sprint Planning topics Why / What / How, work decomposed into items “of one day or less”; artefacts and commitments Product Backlog–Product Goal, Sprint Backlog–Sprint Goal, Increment–Definition of Done; work not meeting the DoD “cannot be released or even presented at the Sprint Review” and returns to the Product Backlog; “multiple Increments may be created within a Sprint”; “The Sprint Review should never be considered a gate to releasing value”; review is a working session, not a presentation; retrospective improvements “may even be added to the Sprint Backlog”; only the PO can cancel a sprint, when the Sprint Goal becomes obsolete; Developers who do the work size it; items Done-able within one sprint are “deemed ready for selection”; “Scrum is founded on empiricism and lean thinking”; burn-downs, burn-ups, cumulative flows “do not replace the importance of empiricism”; the framework is immutable, implementing only parts “is not Scrum”. No mention of user stories, story points or velocity.
- 2020 vs 2017 changes: Development Team removed (one Scrum Team), self-organising → self-managing, Product Goal and commitments added, less prescriptive (three Daily Scrum questions removed). InfoQ https://www.infoq.com/articles/changes-2020-Scrum-guide ; Scrum Inc https://www.scruminc.com/?p=35480. 2017 Guide: “Refinement usually consumes no more than 10% of the capacity of the Development Team”, removed in 2020 (Scrum.org forum https://www.scrum.org/node/40792 and Scrum Inc above).
- Guide length “about thirteen pages”: commonly reported for the 2020 PDF (stated with “zo’n/about”).
- Halving the timeboxes for a two-week sprint (4 h / 2 h / 1.5 h): common practitioner rule of thumb, stated as such; the Guide only says “usually shorter”.
- Takeuchi & Nonaka, “The New New Product Development Game”, HBR Jan 1986 (Honda, Canon, Fuji-Xerox among the cases; rugby vs relay; heading “Moving the Scrum Downfield”); Sutherland took the name for Scrum (Easel, early 1990s). https://www.scrum.org/resources/new-new-product-development-game ; Bain/HBR “The secret history of agile innovation” https://www.bain.com/insights/the-secret-history-of-agile-innovation-hbr/
- Poppendieck seven wastes of software (partially done work, extra features, relearning, handoffs, delays, task switching, defects): M. & T. Poppendieck, Implementing Lean Software Development, 2006 (cited by DZone https://dzone.com/articles/seven-wastes-software). The 2003 book has a slightly different list; the lessons use the 2006 one.
- User story template from Connextra (~2001): widely attributed; the individual authors are not named in the lessons because the attribution could not be confirmed. Three Cs: Ron Jeffries, 2001 (“Essential XP: Card, Conversation, Confirmation”, xp123.com/ronjeffries.com). INVEST: Bill Wake, “INVEST in Good Stories, and SMART Tasks”, 17 Aug 2003, https://xp123.com/
- Planning poker: James Grenning, 2002, popularised by Mike Cohn (Agile Alliance glossary; Mountain Goat Software). Story points from XP “ideal days” renamed because stakeholders were confused that an ideal day took about three real days; Jeffries 2019, “Story Points Revisited”: “I may have invented story points, and if I did, I’m sorry now” (https://ronjeffries.com/articles/019-01ff/story-points/Index.html, site returned 503 during the check; quote confirmed via secondary sources, e.g. https://world.hey.com/itzy/a-story-about-points-with-a-happy-ending-ddec3fc5). Modified Fibonacci 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 20, 40, 100: common planning-poker deck.
- Cone of uncertainty: Boehm, Software Engineering Economics (1981) “funnel curve”; McConnell named it (1997) and gives 0.25×–4× at initial concept. Bossavit notes the ranges are judgement, not measured data — the lesson says so. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cone_of_uncertainty ; https://leanpub.com/read/leprechauns/leanpub-auto-chapter-2-the-cone-of-uncertainty
- Monte Carlo forecasting from throughput: popularised by Troy Magennis and Daniel Vacanti (ActionableAgile). NoEstimates “around 2012” (Woody Zuill, Vasco Duarte): stated with “rond/around”.
- Goodhart’s law, in Strathern’s phrasing “When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure” (Goodhart 1975; Strathern 1997).
- Kanban Guide (Vacanti & Coleman, Dec 2020), https://kanbanguides.org/the-kanban-guide/2020.12 : “a strategy for optimising the flow of value through a process using a visual, pull-based system”; three practices; Definition of Workflow elements (work items, start/finish, states, WIP control, explicit policies, SLE); four flow measures WIP, Throughput, Work Item Age, Cycle Time with the Guide’s definitions; SLE = period + probability, example “85% of work items finish in 8 days”.
- Toyota kanban (Taiichi Ohno, TPS). David J. Anderson, Kanban for knowledge work from 2004 (Microsoft), book “Kanban: Successful Evolutionary Change for Your Technology Business”, 2010.
- Little’s law: J. D. C. Little, “A Proof for the Queuing Formula L = λW”, Operations Research 9(3), 1961. Flow form avg CT = avg WIP / avg TH and its assumptions (conservation of flow, stable WIP at start and end, consistent units; it describes averages, it is not a forecast): Vacanti, Actionable Agile Metrics for Predictability (2015); Scrum.org paper “Little’s Law for Professional Scrum with Kanban” (2018), https://scrumorg-website-prod.s3.amazonaws.com/drupal/2018-05/Little’s Law for Professional Scrum with Kanban.pdf ; Businessmap https://businessmap.io/continuous-flow/littles-law
- Weinberg context-switching rule of thumb (about 20% lost per extra project): G. Weinberg, Quality Software Management Vol. 1 (1992). Stated as an estimate from experience, not a measurement.
- Queue factor u / (1 − u): M/M/1 queue (mean waiting time in queue ∝ ρ/(1 − ρ)); the lessons say “in the simplest queueing model” and use it for the shape only. Reinertsen, The Principles of Product Development Flow (2009), on queues at high utilisation.
- “Stop starting, start finishing”: common Kanban slogan, stated as such.

## Numbers recomputed

- whyagile: 3 + 4 + 1 = 8 months ≈ 34 weeks; 34 / 2 = 17 iterations (recall 1 = 17).
- principles: 12 fields first, +3 = 15, 40 − 15 = 25 never requested.
- scrumbasics: team of 7 = 1 PO + 1 SM + 5 Developers (3 + 1 + 1). Recall 1 = 10 people.
- events: 10 × 15 = 150 min = 2.5 h (recall 1 = 150); halved timeboxes 4 h, 2 h, 1.5 h.
- scrumfail: 8 / 10 = 80% (recall 4 = 80).
- points: 21 + 25 + 18 + 30 + 24 + 22 = 140; 140 / 6 = 23.33 (recall 1 = 23.3, tol 0.1); 120 / 23.3 = 5.1 → 6 sprints; 120 / 30 = 4; 120 / 18 = 6.67 → 7; 4–7 sprints = 8–14 weeks.
- forecast: 60 / 8 = 7.5, 60 / 4 = 15 weeks; 4 × 6 = 24 months, 0.25 × 6 = 1.5 (recall 1 = 24); throughput sum 3+6+5+4+7+5+6+4+5+5 = 50, mean 5; 48 / 5 = 9.6; 48 / 3 = 16. Monte Carlo rechecked with 100,000 runs (bootstrap from the 10 weeks): 50th percentile 10 weeks, 85th 11 weeks, P(≤ 11) ≈ 98%, P(≤ 12) ≈ 99.96% — the text says “50% within 10, 85% within 11, near certainty within 12”.
- wip: 15 / 5 = 3 per person (recall 1 = 3); before: throughput 15 / 6 = 2.5 per week by Little’s law; after: 6 / 2.5 = 2.4 ≈ “about 2.5 weeks”; situation 22 / 6 = 3.7 “almost 4”.
- flowmetrics: 12 / 3 = 4 weeks; 6 / 3 = 2 weeks; recall 20 / 5 = 4 (recall 1 = 4); situation 60 / 15 = 4 weeks, 15 / 15 = 1 week.
- flowsteer: sum of the 20 cycle times = 160, mean 8; 85th percentile by nearest rank = 17th value = 11 days; u/(1 − u): 0.8 → 4, 0.9 → 9 (recall 1 = 9), 0.95 → 19; 9 / 4 = 2.25 “more than twice”.

## Decisions

- Track id `agile`, placed after `project` in `TRACKS.pm`; i18n `track_agile` en “Agile and IT projects”, nl “Agile en IT-projecten”.
- Module level 2: it is an IT-project specialisation and keeps the default pm suggestion on intake/plan for new learners.
- Scrum accountabilities, events and artefacts kept as their English Scrum Guide names in Dutch too (Product Owner, Sprint Goal, Definition of Done…), as Dutch practitioners use them.
- pm.plan already defines Agile, Scrum and Definition of done in its own glossary; the duplicate-term rule is per module, so pm.agile defines them again for its own learners.
- Throughput is defined (in `terms`) in kanban.flowmetrics; backlog.forecast uses it earlier in module order but explains it in the sentence.

Uncertain: Connextra author attribution (left out); exact Jeffries wording (secondary sources only); “about thirteen pages”.
