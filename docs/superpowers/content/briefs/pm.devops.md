# pm.devops — DevOps and delivery (projecten, track agile, level 2)

Index title: en 'DevOps and delivery' / nl 'DevOps en levering'. A module of the Projecten track `agile`, "Agile and IT projects" / "Agile en IT-projecten" (plan: docs/superpowers/plans/2026-11-07-v13-listen-ict-outside-layout.md, section 3). Placed after pm.agile in index.js. The `TRACKS.pm` entry `'agile'` and the `track_agile` strings are identical to the ones in the pm.agile branch, so the merge resolves as a union. Module `level: 2`, like pm.agile.

Format: topic module, 4 topics × 4 levels (Basis, Werkkennis, Gevorderd, Expert), as eng.machining and pm.agile. Worked examples at levels 1–2, faded at 3–4. Every lesson has a `said` line, a situation with pushback (pm style), 5 teach blocks ≤ 90 words, 1 pre question, 4 recall questions, 4–6 terms in the same order in en and nl. Each term is defined once in the module. Situations come from Merijn's work as a junior Lean consultant at manufacturers: MES rollouts and updates, plant IT (ERP/MES links, label printers, scales), OT updates (PLC, SCADA), suppliers' proposals and SLAs.

## Lean links (explicit where real, honest where different)

- Real: pipeline = standard work; stopping at a failed test = jidoka; broken build first = andon cord; flaky test = andon with false alarms; small commits / daily integration = small batches; long branch = work in progress; change lead time ≈ value-stream lead time; change fail rate ≈ first pass yield reversed; local optimisation and value stream mapping of commit-to-production; incident vs problem = quick fix vs root-cause analysis; 5 Whys and A3 from Toyota; standard changes ≈ standard work with a fixed procedure.
- Different: continuous deployment does not fit OT software that controls a running line (maintenance windows, safety, validation); canary needs enough traffic, a factory MES has dozens of users, so roll out per line or shift; complex IT outages often have several contributing causes, not one root (the lesson says so next to 5 Whys).

## Facts and sources (verified 2026-11-08)

- DORA metrics, current definitions and grouping: https://dora.dev/guides/dora-metrics-four-keys/ — change lead time “from committed to version control to deployed in production”; deployment frequency; failed deployment recovery time; change fail rate “ratio of deployments that require immediate intervention”; deployment rework rate “ratio of deployments that are unplanned but happen as a result of an incident in production”; throughput (lead time, deployment frequency, recovery time) vs instability (change fail rate, rework rate).
- History: https://dora.dev/insights/dora-metrics-history/ — 2014 study first used the metrics, 2015 settled on four; availability added 2018, renamed reliability 2021 (2021 report called it the “fifth metric”, DORA now says it is more an operational measure); MTTR became failed deployment recovery time in 2023, limited to failures caused by a change; rework rate added and metrics regrouped in 2024.
- 2023 clusters (Accelerate State of DevOps 2023, https://dora.dev/research/2023/dora-report/ ; table reproduced at https://octopus.com/blog/software-delivery-performance-clusters-2023): elite 18% — on demand, lead time < 1 day, change fail rate 5%, recovery < 1 hour; low 18% — between once a week and once a month, recovery 1–6 months, change fail rate 64%. Lessons state these only with “2023” and “around”. No elite cluster in 2022 (Google Cloud blog on the 2023 report; newsletter.getdx.com summary).
- 2025: report renamed “State of AI-assisted Software Development”; seven team profiles replace the low/medium/high/elite clusters (Google blog https://blog.google/technology/developers/dora-report-2025/ ; report PDF https://services.google.com/fh/files/misc/2025_state_of_ai_assisted_software_development.pdf ; reviews by RedMonk and Splunk). Lessons only say “seven named team profiles based on performance, stability and well-being”; no profile names or percentages.
- DORA history and people: Forsgren, Humble, Kim, Accelerate (IT Revolution, 2018); Google acquired DORA in December 2018 (stated as “since late 2018”). Survey-based, mostly software organisations: lessons call the figures estimates, not measurements.
- Trunk-based development: https://dora.dev/capabilities/trunk-based-development/ — “three or fewer active branches”, “merge branches to trunk at least once a day”, “don’t have code freezes and don’t have integration phases”, branches “typically last no more than a few hours”.
- Heavyweight change approval: https://dora.dev/capabilities/streamlining-change-approval/ citing the 2019 State of DevOps report — CAB / senior-manager approval has “a negative impact on software delivery performance”; “no evidence was found” that a more formal external review lowers change fail rates; mechanism via bigger, less frequent batches.
- J-curve: DORA / State of DevOps 2018 (“J-curve of transformation”). Stated as “DORA beschrijft / DORA describes”, without a year in the lesson.
- DevOps origin: Patrick Debois, first DevOpsDays, Ghent, October 2009 (stated as “around 2009”).
- Continuous delivery: Jez Humble & David Farley, Continuous Delivery (Addison-Wesley, 2010). Delivery vs deployment distinction as in that book and common usage.
- Test pyramid: Mike Cohn, Succeeding with Agile (2009); “what experts argue about” reflects the ongoing debate (e.g. testing trophy / more integration tests), stated without naming people.
- Blue-green deployment: Martin Fowler, 1 March 2010, https://martinfowler.com/bliki/BlueGreenDeployment.html — two environments, router switch, fast rollback, database caveat.
- Canary release: Danilo Sato, https://martinfowler.com/bliki/CanaryRelease.html (2014); name from canaries in coal mines. Not cited by name in the lesson.
- Feature toggles: Pete Hodgson, “Feature Toggles (aka Feature Flags)”, martinfowler.com, October 2017 — release, experiment, ops and permissioning toggles; the lesson uses release vs ops. 2^10 = 1,024 combinations is arithmetic.
- Expand-contract (parallel change): Danilo Sato, https://martinfowler.com/bliki/ParallelChange.html (2014); same four steps as Fowler’s blue-green database advice.
- Knight Capital, 1 August 2012: SEC order (Release No. 70694, 2013) — a repurposed flag reactivated old “Power Peg” code on one of eight servers that had not received the new code; losses reported at about $440–460 million in about 45 minutes. Lessons say “zo’n 440 miljoen dollar / some 440 million dollars” and “about 45 minutes”.
- ITIL: origin CCTA (UK government) late 1980s; ITIL 4 Foundation published February 2019 by AXELOS; PeopleCert acquired AXELOS in 2021. Definitions (ITIL 4 glossary, via ManageEngine https://www.manageengine.com/products/service-desk/itsm/itil-4-glossary-terms.html and InvGate https://www.invgate.com/guides/problem-management): incident “an unplanned interruption to a service or reduction in the quality of a service”; problem “a cause, or potential cause, of one or more incidents”; known error “a problem that has been analysed but has not been resolved”; change “the addition, modification, or removal of anything that could have a direct or indirect effect on services”. Change enablement and standard / normal / emergency changes: https://itsm.tools/change-enablement/ ; AWS “Change enablement in ITIL 4” https://docs.aws.amazon.com/wellarchitected/latest/change-enablement-in-the-cloud/change-enablement-in-itil4.html
- Blameless postmortems: Beyer et al., Site Reliability Engineering (O’Reilly / Google, 2016), chapter 15 “Postmortem Culture: Learning from Failure”; SRE Workbook chapter 10 https://sre.google/workbook/postmortem-culture/
- 5 Whys: Taiichi Ohno, Toyota Production System (1978/1988). A3: Toyota problem-solving report on A3 paper (Shook, Managing to Learn, 2008). Andon: TPS; the line stops at the fixed position if the problem is not solved within the cycle (simplified to “within the cycle”).
- Watermelon SLA: common ITSM term (“green outside, red inside”), stated as such.
- Availability arithmetic: 30 days × 24 h = 720 h; 0.5% → 3.6 h; 0.1% → 0.72 h = 43.2 min.
- GDPR (AVG) and test data: stated generally (“sets requirements”), no article numbers.
- Pharma validation: stated generally (“demonstrably tested according to fixed rules, with documentation”); no GAMP/Annex 11 claims.

## Answer balance

68 multiple-choice questions per language (16 pre + 52 recall). Answer positions 15 / 20 / 17 / 16 over A–D, identical in en and nl. The correct option is the longest in 15 (nl) and 19 (en) of 68 questions, never by more than 25% over the next longest. Distractors are common misconceptions (e.g. “a short code freeze for safety”, “a higher fail rate comes with deploying more often”) rather than throwaways.
